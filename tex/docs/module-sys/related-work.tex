\section{Related work}
\label{fexpr-comparison}

The techniques applied in this paper achieve results similar to systems using
fexprs~\cite{fexpr-shutt,kernel-report,fexpr-pe}.
Fexprs are a different kind of metaprogramming mechanism: they unify functions
and macros as a single kind of object%,
\footnote{
    The exact definition of the term \emph{fexpr} varies throughout
    the literature. Here it is used in the sense of an applicative object
    that may choose whether to have its operands evaluated before
    application~\cite{fexpr-shutt}.
}, which, depending on the implementation,
can be first-class and as flexible as unhygienic macros,%
~\cite{kohlbecker1986syntactic,fexpr-shutt} and can be implemented
in a performant way~\cite{fexpr-pe}. The core mechanism of fexprs is
to tag applicative objects with evaluation metadata and consult that metadata
when evaluating a combination to decide whether to evaluate the operands.
Fexprs are first-class regardless of their evaluation semantics.%
~\cite{fexpr-shutt,kernel-report}

Similar to modern fexpr-based systems, ILD's macro expansion facility matches
unhygienic LISP macros in expressive power while providing first-class
status and runtime semantics with no separate expansion phase for macros.

To our knowledge, the closest prior work to this paper is the Kernel
programming language~\cite{kernel-report}. The semantics of Kernel is based on
fexprs and features first-class macros and statically-scoped
bindings~\cite{kernel-report,fexpr-shutt}. ILD differs from Kernel in
making macro expansion explicit at the callsite rather than
as a property of the applied object.
We argue that callsite-triggered macro expansion is simpler to implement and
reason about while remaining just as expressive.
